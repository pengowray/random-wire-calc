How the score works

Band scoreSWR across the band
Near 100Well within your tuner's SWR limit
50About at the tuner's limit
Near 0Well beyond the tuner's limit

For each wire length, the calculator models the feedpoint impedance at several frequencies across each selected band, including the band edges. It divides the impedance by the matching ratio (9 for a 9:1 unun) and calculates the SWR on 50 ohm coax. Each frequency gets a mark from 0 to 1 compared with the tuner's limit. The band score is the average mark across the band, times 100. The overall score is the average of the band scores.

Suggested lengths are the best-scoring lengths in the length range. A length within the minimum spacing of a higher-scoring suggestion is left out, so near-identical lengths are listed once.

Model accuracy and limits

SWR figures are typically within 15 to 20% of NEC-2 simulations. The lengths where the SWR peaks and dips line up closely with the NEC results. The results have not been compared with real-world measurements.

Not modelled:

  • coax loss
  • common-mode current on the coax (the coax shield acting as part of the counterpoise)
  • unun losses
  • nearby objects
  • bends, and wire shapes other than the simulated one
  • variations in real ground

Ground radials are modelled as a plain 15 Ω resistance. That setting was not simulated in NEC.

Method: the wire and the counterpoise are treated as two lossy open-ended transmission lines in series, a standard approximation. Radiation resistance comes from the textbook formula for a sinusoidal current distribution. The constants were fitted to NEC-2 simulations (PyNEC) of a sloping end-fed wire over average ground: feedpoint 1.5 m up, wire rising to 4, 8 or 12 m and then running level, counterpoise 2.5, 5 or 10 m.

What the simulations showed:

  • Wire height made little difference.
  • Counterpoise length made a large difference. A counterpoise close to half a wavelength on a band raises the SWR on that band at every wire length.
  • Through a 9:1 unun, the worst SWR is usually at lengths just longer than each multiple of a half wavelength, where the impedance is low and strongly capacitive. It is not exactly at the half-wave points.

Simulation details and data

Notes about random wire (long wire) antennas

  • Usually longer than 1/4λ at the lowest operating frequency
  • Suited for low-power operating
  • Aim to be non-resonant on any particular frequency
  • Usually fed with a 9:1 unun
  • A counterpoise (or multiple counterpoises) is recommended
  • Typically requires an antenna tuner to achieve a match
  • Efficiency decreases when the wire is installed closer to earth
  • Check with other sources for authoritative information
